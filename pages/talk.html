Overcoming Trials

Apr 19 2026 4th ward

Good afternoon brothers and sisters. Autumn and I have been in the 4th ward since we got married a couple years ago, and we've loved our time here! We are currently fostering a couple of kids, and because one of them is three, we'll be moving to family ward next week. Although we're excited for our next adventure, we're sad to leave this Stake behind.

[As sister Palmer has already mentioned], the topic we were assigned to speak on comes from President Eyering's and Sister Runia's talks about how God uses our afflictions for our benefit. I would like to share some examples of this that have inspired me.

Job

The first person that comes to my mind when it comes to suffering trials for no apparent reason is Job. He started as a man who had it all, but was permitted by God to lose it all at the hands of Satan, who believed that the only reason Job was a faithful man was that God had blessed him so much.

First, armies and a fire came and destroyed his business, killed his servants, and plundered his possessions. Second, all his children were killed when the wind blew down the house where they were gathered together. Third, he began to be covered in painful sores from head to foot. His response? "Naked came I out of my mother's womb, and naked shall I return thither: the Lord gave, and the Lord hath taken away; blessed be the name of the Lord."

Job is a perfect example of recognizing that none of the good things we have in life are truly ours. They are given to us for a time as gifts from God, and we are in no way entitled to having those gifts for any permanent period of time. Job knew this, and, despite having lost it all, he still chose to praise God.

Years ago, when my mom asked my teenage sister to do her chores, she responded, "It's not like you do anything around here." Yeah... we still give her a hard time about that one. Like teenagers who take the efforts of their parents for granted, we often forget that everything we have, even our very existence, is the direct result of God's undeserved love for us. Considering everything he's done for us, when we complain about our trials, we are like a teenager complaining about the rules of the house instead of being grateful for having a house to live in at all.

How can we have a mindset like Job? It seems impossible! But I think the key is gratitude. Taking time to express gratitude for things in our lives can pull us out of negative thinking and teach us to avoid taking things for granted.

Betsie and the Fleas

In a book called The Hiding Place, Corrie and her older sister Betsie were in a concentration camp because their family had been hiding Jews in their home. While they were locked in a flea-infested room, and were unsure how they were going to handle living in such horrible conditions, the answer came to them from 1 Thessalonians 5:18, which reads, "Give thanks in all circumstances." So, Betsie began to pray. She thanked God for the jammed, crammed, stuffed, packed, suffocating crowds, and for the fleas. Corrie interrupted, "Betsie, there's no way even God can make me grateful for a flea."

As time went on, they began to host secret bible studies with a growing number of the other women at the camp. They were very careful at first because of the number of guards patrolling the camp, but the guards never seemed to bother inspecting their room.

One day, they figured out why when they tried to get their supervisor to come in and answer some questions they had about their work assignments. The lady refused to enter the room, saying, "That place is crawling with fleas!" They realized in that moment that while they had struggled to feel gratitude for the fleas, it was because of the fleas that their bible studies had gone unnoticed by the guards, and it was their bible studies that gave them hope.

Corrie and Betsie's story about the fleas shows us that many of the things that bring us suffering might actually be part of a greater plan that God has authored for our benefit. We may not understand his ways yet, but we can rest assured that he will make it so that "all things shall work together for [our] good."

Job's Prayers of Confusion and Suffering

While it's important to express gratitude for everything we have, and in all circumstances in which God might place us, this doesn't mean we need to sacrifice our honesty. Once again, Job sets an example for us. Job's prayers to God are filled with his frustration with his circumstances and his confusion about God's motives. Job tells God exactly how he feels while still acknowledging the wisdom and sovereignty of God.

For a long time, I've been struggling to feel a meaningful connection to God in my prayers. One of the things that has helped me the most is to recognize that one of the purposes of prayer is to pour out our heart to God. This means sharing what's troubling us, even if it sounds a little resentful. As I've made an effort to share the things that have been bothering me lately, or that have been making my life hard, I've noticed that I've begun to see prayers as less of a sleep-inducing chore and more of a great tool that pulls down comfort and guidance from heaven. This has been a witness to me that God hears our prayers, and that when we come to him with our problems, he visits us with solutions.

Nephi

Another example of triumph in trials is found in the most-read section of The Book of Mormon. Nephi's life was full of hardship. He had to leave civilization with his family to go camping for several years in the desert. During that time, they had to hunt for food and ate most of it raw because God told them not to light fires. His older brothers were very abusive and even tried to kill him on multiple occasions. After travelling through the desert, they sailed across the ocean, not having any idea where they were going. After arriving in the land God promised them, his brothers began plotting to kill him once again. Yet, in the first few verses of the book, as he introduces himself and his story, he claims to have been "highly favored of the Lord", despite all his afflictions.

One of the things that stood out to me about Nephi is his ability to forgive his brothers. Although much of his suffering was heaped upon him by his brothers' hands, he was able to let go of the hatred he was surely tempted to feel for them. Instead of responding to their oppression with aggression and violence, he promptly forgave them each time and urged them to get right with God.

Some of the suffering we experience in life has and will come at the hands of others. When we choose to get angry, we are making our own pain worse. When we seek revenge, we often end up doing things we end up regretting later on. Thus, one of the most important lessons we can learn when we have to endure the unkindness of others is to be more forgiving.

In a world where opinions have become very polarized, where the internet makes it easier than ever to say hateful things without having to face whoever reads it, and where the future is more uncertain than ever, we need to be able to forgive those who have wronged us, and respond with love and concern. It's the only way to calm the building tension.

Another thing we learn from Nephi's example is the value of going and doing. There were several times when everyone else around him was complaining about the tough situation they were in, but he firmly resolved to accomplish "the thing which the Lord had commanded them". When they were having trouble obtaining the scriptures written on brass plates, his brothers gave up, but he bravely walked into Jerusalem, trusting that God would help him. When he and his brothers were confused about the meaning of their father's vision, his brothers complained, but he asked God for a greater understanding, and received his own vision. When Nephi's family ran out of food, they all sat in their tents feeling defeated, but Nephi found himself a stick and jerry-rigged a hunting bow.

What we should each borrow from Nephi is a resolve to conquer trials instead of being conquered by them.

Nephi's mindset is also known as a proactive mindset. While a reactive mindset means letting our circumstances dictate how we respond, a proactive mindset is about focusing on what we can do to affect our circumstances.

In his book about 7 habits, Stephen Covey offers a mental model to help us think about what it means to be proactive. Everything that worries us is in a circle he calls the "circle of concern." Within that circle is a much smaller circle he calls the "circle of influence." This circle contains all the things we can actually do something about. He pointed out that we often spend a lot of our time worrying about things that are outside of our circle of influence. When we do this, our circle of influence shrinks, meaning we end up having less control! But when we focus on the things that we can influence, our circle of influence begins to grow, so that we are capable of solving even more problems.

Joseph

Few people lived this principle better than Joseph, the interpreter of dreams. Out of jealousy, his older brothers sold him as a slave to some passing merchants as a way of getting rid of him without having to get their hands too dirty. Joseph was taken far away from his home and was put to work as a servant in Potiphar's house.

Joseph certainly missed his home, his parents, and his freedom, but despite this, he managed to avoid falling into despair and to focus on his new responsibilities as a servant. He chose to work hard and do his best, which earned him the respect of others.

Even though Joseph continued to suffer challenging setbacks, he continued to pick himself up and rise above his situation by doing the best he could with whatever circumstances he found himself in.

What can we learn from Joseph? We can learn that our trials don't determine our future; we determine our future. Or, more precisely, God determines our future, and if we do our best with whatever we have, we are giving God more to work with. Our diligent efforts, when combined with God's power and foresight, will unlock possibilities we would have never considered.

God Helped Me In My Classes

While I was still in college, there was this one professor that nearly all of my classmates and I disliked a lot. His teaching methods were infuriating, and he often did a lot of things that seemed unfair or nonsensical. He also didn't seem to really know what he was talking about half the time. In our other classes, my classmates and I would rant about him behind his back, sharing with each other all the things we noticed in his classes that we were frustrated about. We had all decided that when the end of semester surveys came, we would make sure he knew that he was in the wrong.

At some point through the semester, I had a distinct impression that what we were doing was not Christlike. I'm sure it came from the Holy Ghost. I realized in that moment that sharing our frustrations about him with each other was not going to make him a better professor, it wasn't going to help us perform better as students, and it was draining me of energy. I made a commitment to stop blaming him for my experience in his classes, and to stop seeing him as the enemy.

What followed was a huge shift in my experience in his classes. Because I was taking responsibility for my own education, I began to feel like I was learning more and understanding things better. Because I stopped dwelling on my frustrations and gossiping with the other students, I started to feel more lighthearted and optimistic about what the rest of the semester would be like. Because I chose to stop seeing him as the enemy, I grew to like him more as a person. Because I chose to be curious rather than dismissive of his efforts to teach us, I began to notice things about how he taught us and prepared us that impressed me. I realized that my earlier negativity towards him had been preventing me from seeing anything good about him and the classes he was teaching.

At the end of the semester, when I overheard some of my classmates grumbling about him, I realized just how much my overall experience with the class had totally shifted. God had taken my efforts to be positive and to claim responsibility, and used them to transform some deeply frustrating classes into interesting and enjoyable classes.

This experience showed me that when we put in effort to do what we know is right and to work hard, God helps us have a totally different experience. This is an example of how he can turn trials into blessings.

Ephraim and Manasseh

Near the end of Joseph's story, after having been appointed second-in-command to the pharaoh himself, he had two sons, one named Manasseh, and the other named Ephraim.

In Hebrew, Manasseh means "forgetting" or "evaporating." Joseph chose this name for his firstborn son because the Lord made him to forget his past and his hardship, bringing him greater peace of mind in his afflictions.

The name Ephraim means "doubly fruitful." Joseph chose this name for his second son because the Lord blessed his efforts, making him prosper even though he had been so powerless.

The meanings of these names inspire me because they remind me that God will comfort me in my trials and consecrate my efforts for my good. Those names also inspire me because they give me hope for what it will be like to reach the end of my life, look back on all my trials, and see the hand of God in the details.

Christ

The final example I'd like to share is the example of our Savior. His plate was more full than any of us can imagine because of the great responsibilities he had. As the chosen Savior of the world, his purpose is to redeem all mankind from our fallen state, paving the way of escape from physical and spiritual death. To say that this was a great undertaking is a great understatement. Such a task would definitely overwhelm the best of us if it were set in front of us.

Although he had every reason to doubt whether he would be able to fulfill God's will for his life, he pushed through and accomplished his role as the prophesied Christ with perfect diligence. He always did his Father's will all the way to the very end.

I'm sure it's probably impossible for any of us to be just as perfectly obedient as Jesus, but that shouldn't stop us from trying. Our trials are perfect opportunities to learn what God's will is for our lives, and to lean into the lessons he's trying to teach us and the opportunities he's trying to prepare us for.

One problem we tend to have, at least I do, is that we tend to spend more time focusing on how hard our trials are for us than on what God would have us do.

Knowing our human nature, Christ taught us specifically what to do when we're feeling overwhelmed with life. When Christ taught his sermon on the mount, he was talking to regular people like us, who have lots going on in their lives and have lots of day-to-day things to worry about. To them he said, "Take no thought, saying, What shall we eat? or, What shall we drink? or, Wherewithal shall we be clothed?...for your heavenly Father knoweth that ye have need of all these things. But seek ye first the kingdom of God, and his righteousness; and all these things shall be added unto you." Or in other words, when you're stressed about the hardship you've been called to pass through, make God your first priority, and he will make sure that things work out the way they need to.

Because of Christ's perfect life and perfect sacrifice, he alone is able to comfort us and strengthen us in whatever difficulties we face. Because he suffered all things, he can bring us peace in the storm. Because he conquered all things, he can give us the strength to conquer our things. Because of him, we can look forward to the day when all things will be made right.

Conclusion

I testify that our bad days can be turned into blessings as we turn to our Savior, express gratitude for all we have, and rely on his strength and on his plan for us. In the name of Jesus Christ, amen.
