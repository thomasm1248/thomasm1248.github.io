Why I started daily planning

Oct 15, 2026

Is it a waste of time to make plans?

I used to think so. Why bother making a plan for my entire day if my plans are just going to have to change? If you make a plan, it's just going to crumble once reality takes its unpredictable course.

I continued with this belief until an interaction I had with Muse. When I installed it on my phone and began describing to it my unfulfilled desires, it immediately began making plans and getting the ball rolling, and I was like, "Why didn't I think of that?" -- "Why didn't I think of just... starting??" As I wrestled with this question, I learned something new about myself: I have a surprisingly strong bias towards inaction. I have an ever-growing backlog of things I'd like to do, but I seem to be stuck in the mindset that each thing on that list is to be done "later". The missing link was that I didn't seem to have a system for turning "later" into "now".

So I decided to make some tweaks to the methods by which I direct my life. I decided that I was going to focus on one thing: turning thoughts into action. Rather than simply floating through life, wondering why my bucketlist items never wandered their way into the present moment, I decided to take a more proactive approach. But how?

I eventually decided that my central problem is that I had a bad habit of simply going with the flow of each day.Instead of deciding ahead of time what I was going to do with my time, I was letting the needs of each moment dictate what I spent my time on. Since there's not really any particular reason why any given moment needs to be tied up with items from my backlog of tasks and wishes, it never naturally happened. The only way to pull backlog items into the present moment was to do it with intention.

But, like I said, I've always resisted making plans because they tend to fall apart. So I asked myself, "So what if it falls apart? What would actually happen?" That's when I had an epiphany: even if a plan falls apart, it was probably still worth the effort to make. Here's why:

  • A derailed plan is typically better than nothing.
  • Every time a plan breaks is an opportunity to learn the valuable skill of adjusting plans.
  • Whenever a plan fails, you learn how to make better plans that are more realistic and more flexible.
  • Making and adjusting plans trains your mind how to plan subconsciously and naturally.

I began starting each morning by splitting an index card into boxes, one box for every hour of my free time. Then I add the things from my calendar. I finish by filling the rest of the empty space with plans to get started on things I've been thinking about but have never started. Throughout the day, I adjust the plan as needed. At the end of the day, I reflect on how my plan evolved as my day unfolded. I'm getting a lot more of my things done now that I'm deliberately putting them in my day.
